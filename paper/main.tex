\title{Recovery Lives in $u$, Conditioning Acts in $r$:\\
Where Arbitrary-Scale Super-Resolution Gains Come From}
\name{Anonymous authors}
\address{Anonymous institution}

\begin{abstract}
Arbitrary-scale super-resolution (ASISR) networks pair an image encoder with an implicit decoder that also receives the target pixel size, the \emph{cell}. Decoder and cell designs keep multiplying, yet their gains shrink at large scales. We explain this with two frequency coordinates. Per-band recovery is a function of frequency normalised by the low-resolution (LR) Nyquist limit, $u$. Four decoder designs on a shared encoder give the same recovery to within 0.02, on crops and on full benchmark images. Longer training, a stronger encoder (RDN, SwinIR) and a larger decoder MLP raise recovery only for $u\gtrsim0.75$, even under a blur degradation that leaves sub-Nyquist bands incomplete for interpolation. The cell, by contrast, acts on the output as a low-pass filter in high-resolution frequency $r$. From the orthogonality principle we derive that its cost is the recovered energy it filters away. A fixed $r$ moves to larger $u$ as the scale grows, where little has been recovered, so the cell's leverage vanishes. Doubling the cell costs 2.3\,dB at $\times2$, 0.13\,dB at $\times4$ and nothing measurable at $\times6$. Retraining without the cell input still costs about 0.1\,dB at every scale on standard benchmarks. This loss does not depend on scale and lies above the Nyquist limit: the cell helps training even where it no longer acts at inference. All predictions were registered before the experiments, and we report those that failed.
\end{abstract}
\begin{keywords}
Arbitrary-scale super-resolution, implicit neural representation, frequency analysis, scale conditioning
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
LIIF~\cite{chen2021liif} reconstructs an image at continuous coordinates by decoding local encoder features together with the query offset and the target cell size. Many decoders have followed: local Fourier estimators~\cite{lee2022lte}, integrated positional encoding~\cite{liu2021ipe}, implicit attention~\cite{cao2023ciaosr}, neural operators~\cite{wei2023srno}, Gaussian splatting~\cite{hu2024gaussiansr} and filtered heat fields~\cite{becker2025thera}. Their gains are small. A recent benchmark reports saturation, with improvements that follow model size, compute and data rather than decoder form~\cite{nasir2026empirical}. A spectral decoder reports leads over strong baselines that vanish at large scales, without an explanation~\cite{shad2026gblsr}. Frequency analyses of SR networks characterise low-pass behaviour~\cite{fsds2024} and sinusoidal responses~\cite{kawai2026frequency}, and study how frequency representations transfer across resolutions~\cite{wang2026scaleanchoring}. None of them locates \emph{where} in frequency a decoder or the cell can still change the output, or why that room shrinks with scale.

We answer with two coordinates. For scale $s$ and HR frequency $r$ (cycles per HR pixel), $u=2sr$ is the frequency relative to the LR Nyquist limit. We make three claims.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Recovery lives in $u$.} Per-band recovery $R(u)$ does not depend on decoder design. What moves it is learned capacity (training time, encoder or decoder MLP), and only above $u\approx0.75$.
\item \textbf{Conditioning acts in $r$.} Changing the cell filters the native output by an approximately scale-independent low-pass $w(r)$. Its cost equals the recovered energy at the filtered frequencies (Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}), so the cell's leverage decays with scale.
\item \textbf{Consequences.} Decoder form is not a lever, whereas capacity is, wherever it sits. At $\times3$ and above the cell no longer acts as an inference-time control, although it still helps training.
\end{enumerate}
That the cell input can sometimes hurt is known~\cite{liu2021ipe}. Our contribution is a quantitative account of where decoder- and cell-side changes can act at all. Each experiment fixed its code, predictions and scoring before running; Table~\ref{tab:gates} lists every outcome.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Models.} LIIF, LTE~\cite{lee2022lte} and SC-INR (an LTE variant whose cell enters through an analytic sinc weight) share an EDSR-baseline encoder~\cite{lim2017edsr} and the LIIF recipe: DIV2K~\cite{agustsson2017div2k}, scales drawn from $\mathcal U(1,4)$, 1000 epochs, three seeds. GaussianSR~\cite{hu2024gaussiansr} adds a fourth decoder on the same encoder. For matched comparisons we retrain LIIF for 200 epochs with one recipe, changing only one component: an RDN~\cite{zhang2018rdn} or SwinIR~\cite{liang2021swinir} encoder, a smaller decoder MLP ($2\times64$ instead of $4\times256$), or no cell input.

\textbf{Data.} We use DIV2K validation images 0869--0900, not used for any model selection, and full Set14~\cite{zeyde2010single}, BSD100~\cite{martin2001database} and Urban100~\cite{huang2015urban}. LR inputs come from anti-aliased bicubic downsampling at $\times\{2,3,4,6\}$; $\times6$ lies outside the training range. Frequency analyses use centre $240^2$ crops unless stated otherwise; Table~\ref{tab:budget} reports standard Y-channel PSNR on full benchmark images.

\textbf{Measures.} After a 4-pixel shave, the error and the target spectrum are split by Chebyshev frequency radius into $u$-bins of width $0.25$. We define $R(u)=1-\mathrm{MSE}_u/E_u$, where $E_u$ is the target energy in the bin. To probe the cell, we multiply the native cell by $m\in[\frac12,2]$ at fixed input and query.

\section{Recovery lives in $u$}
\label{sec:u}
Below the LR Nyquist limit, networks and bicubic interpolation both recover nearly everything (Fig.~\ref{fig:rec}a). The networks' advantage lies in $0.75\lesssim u\lesssim2$: at $u=1$, $R=0.68$ against $0.20$ for bicubic; at $u=1.5$, $0.36$ against $0.04$. Curves for different scales nearly coincide. This is partly generic: bicubic interpolation collapses in $u$ too, reflecting roughly scale-invariant image statistics and a degradation defined on the LR grid. The collapse is also only approximate (Table~\ref{tab:gates}, first row). Our claims therefore rest on which components can \emph{move} $R(u)$.

\textbf{Decoder design does not.} On a shared encoder, LIIF, LTE, SC-INR and GaussianSR differ by at most $0.010$ in every bin (95\% image-bootstrap interval $[0.007,0.016]$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rec}b). On full Set14, BSD100 and Urban100 images the spread stays below $0.019$ in all 54 dataset--scale--bin cells. Accordingly, their PSNR differs by only 0.03--0.06\,dB.

\textbf{Learned capacity does, above $u\approx0.75$.} Three interventions change $R(u)$, and all three act only there (Fig.~\ref{fig:rec}c). From epoch 100 to 1000, $R$ rises by up to $0.045$ for $u\ge1$ while changing by at most $0.008$ below $u<0.75$. With the recipe fixed, an RDN encoder raises $R$ by up to about $0.05$ for $u\ge1$ in both seeds, and SwinIR does likewise, with $u<0.75$ unchanged. Shrinking the decoder MLP lowers $R$ over the same range. Table~\ref{tab:budget} gives the PSNR effects.

\textbf{The boundary is the Nyquist limit, not linear recoverability.} We retrained EDSR, RDN and no-cell LIIF under a second degradation: Gaussian blur ($\sigma=0.6s$ HR pixels) followed by bicubic resizing without anti-aliasing. Here bicubic interpolation leaves sub-Nyquist bands incomplete ($R=0.71$ at $u=0.5$, $0.36$ at $u=0.75$, $\times4$). We had predicted that capacity would then also act below the Nyquist limit. It does not. Even the EDSR-baseline model recovers these bands almost fully ($0.997$ at $u=0.5$), and RDN raises $R$ only from $u\approx0.75$ on, by up to $0.058$, at every scale. The RDN gain on the DIV2K images (+0.19 to +0.33\,dB) also reproduces.

\section{Conditioning acts in $r$}
\label{sec:r}
Let $P_1$ be the native output and $P_m$ the output with the cell multiplied by $m$, both projected onto band $b$. Empirically,
\begin{equation}
P_{m,b}\approx w_m(r_b)\,P_{1,b},\qquad w_m(r)\in\mathbb R,
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
where $w_2$ is a low-pass in HR frequency with its minimum at $r\approx0.3$ for all three LIIF-family decoders (Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}a). With $e_b=T_b-P_{1,b}$ the native error, Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} gives the exact expansion
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{MSE}_b=2(1-w)\,\mathrm{Re}\langle e_b,P_{1,b}\rangle+(1-w)^2\|P_{1,b}\|^2 .
\label{eq:leverage}
\end{equation}
For an output equal to the conditional mean, the orthogonality principle gives $\langle e_b,P_{1,b}\rangle=0$. The first-order term then vanishes, which is what a calibrated native cell means. The emitted energy also equals the recovered energy, $\|P_{1,b}\|^2=R_bE_b$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{MSE}(s)\approx\sum_r\big(1-w(r)\big)^2\,R(2sr)\,E(r).
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
The two coordinates meet here: the cell supplies a filter in $r$, the network supplies recovery in $u=2sr$. Because $R$ falls with $u$, the same filter removes less and less recovered energy as $s$ grows.

\textbf{Checking the derivation.} The networks are trained with an $L_1$ loss, so orthogonality can only hold approximately. On the DIV2K images we find $|\langle e_b,P_{1,b}\rangle|/\|P_{1,b}\|^2\le0.15$ in all 105 bins with $u<2$, for all three decoders. For $m=2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} predicts the measured cost at $\times3$ and $\times4$ to within a factor of 0.71--0.84. It underpredicts consistently, because the cell also changes the output beyond a per-band scalar. At $\times2$ the shortfall reaches about $2\times$ on Set14. For small mismatch ($m=4/3$) the first-order term dominates and the approximation fails.

\textbf{Consequences.} The cost of a doubled cell peaks at $r\approx0.25$ for every LIIF-family model, seed and scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}b), and it tracks recovered energy (Spearman 0.77--0.78). It decays monotonically with scale: 2.3\,dB at $\times2$, 0.40\,dB at $\times3$, 0.13\,dB at $\times4$ and $0.01$\,dB at $\times6$ (interval includes zero; Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}c). The decay also holds for GaussianSR and for an external RDN-BTC model~\cite{pak2023btc}, although their cost peaks at other $r$. The native cell is close to optimal: no fixed multiplier improves on it by more than $0.017$\,dB.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} tests the consequences by retraining. Stronger encoders help at every scale. Shrinking the decoder MLP hurts at every scale (0.24--0.28\,dB), and again only for $u\gtrsim0.75$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rec}c). Before training we had predicted that it would be harmless at large scales; that failed. The decoder MLP thus stores part of the above-Nyquist prior. The accurate reading is that decoder \emph{form} is not a lever, since four designs give identical $R(u)$, while learned capacity is, wherever it sits.

Figure~\ref{fig:qual} shows a typical case: no model recovers the stripe pattern well above the LR Nyquist limit, and the differences between models concentrate on edges close to it.

Removing the cell input separates two roles of the cell. As an inference-time control, its leverage vanishes from $\times3$ on, exactly as Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} predicts. A model \emph{trained} without it, however, loses about 0.1\,dB at every scale, consistently in both seeds. On the DIV2K images the loss is smaller (0.10\,dB at $\times2$, then indistinguishable from zero), where our registered prediction failed only narrowly. The loss does not shrink with scale, and on full Set14 images it sits above the Nyquist limit ($R$ lower by 0.01--0.02 for $u\ge2$). Supplying the cell during multi-scale training therefore helps the network learn the above-Nyquist prior, even though the same input has no measurable effect at large scales at inference. Our account explains the second fact, not the first.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}
We study bicubic and Gaussian-blur degradations, LIIF-style training, and convolutional and Transformer encoders. The matched arms use two seeds and short schedules. The filter model holds for large mismatch only, and the frequency at which the cell acts depends on the decoder family. The training benefit of the cell input is measured but not explained. Within these limits, ASISR gains come from what the network learns above the LR Nyquist limit. Learned capacity, not decoder form, sets that prior. The cell is an HR-frequency control whose reach is the recovered energy it filters. Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} thus tells in advance at which scales a new cell or footprint design can matter: only where $R(2sr)$ remains substantial at the frequencies the design alters.
